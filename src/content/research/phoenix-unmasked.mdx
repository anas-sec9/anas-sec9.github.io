A while back I took apart [BugSleep](/research/bugsleep-unmasked), MuddyWater's older backdoor, and
wrote detections from what I actually found instead of from a vendor PDF. It went well enough that I
wanted a harder one —
something newer, and ideally something where the public reporting and the sample would give me a
reason to argue with myself.

I got exactly that.

This time the target was **Phoenix**, the backdoor MuddyWater ran through 2025 against government
targets across the Middle East, North Africa, and a few other places. Every published write-up I
could find described it the same way: AES-encrypted. I went in expecting to crack AES. I did not
crack AES, because there wasn't any — at least not where everyone said it was. That disagreement is
the spine of this whole post, and I'm going to show you exactly how I got there so you can check my
work.

Same rules as last time: everything happened in my own isolated lab, and where I was wrong on the
first pass I left it in and corrected it. Pretending you nailed it first try is how you end up
sounding like a press release.

## Who's MuddyWater, and what's Phoenix?

MuddyWater is an Iran-nexus crew — you'll also see them as Earth Vetala, Static Kitten, Mercury, or
Seedworm. They're tied to Iranian intelligence, they lean heavily on phishing, and they spend a lot
of time on targets in my part of the world.

Phoenix is a backdoor they pushed hard in 2025 — Group-IB tied it to a campaign hitting 100+
government organizations. It's named after the string sitting in its own debug path (the developers
left `phoenix.pdb` baked into the build, complete with a `phonix` typo in the folder name — hold
that thought, it becomes useful later). Functionally it's a straightforward implant: register with a
C2, take commands, run them. The interesting parts are how it's delivered and how it's hidden.

## The lab

Nothing exotic. A Windows analysis VM (FLARE-VM), fully isolated on a host-only network, with
FakeNet-NG standing in for the whole internet so the malware talks to me instead of its real C2.
Snapshot before detonation, revert after. Usual toolbox: DIE, PEStudio, FLOSS, CAPA, Ghidra,
HollowsHunter, procdump, and a lot of small Python scripts.

I started from the phishing document. Two hashes matter up front:

```
Maldoc   40dead1e1d83107698ff96bce9ea52236803b15b63fb0002e0b55af71a9b5e05
Lure     Hebrew-language "New protocols and rules.doc", impersonating netivtech
```

## The document: a macro that hides its own payload

`oleid` and `olevba` told the story fast. The doc has a VBA macro that runs on open
(`Document_Open`), and it's carrying a big blob of hex-encoded data inside a UserForm textbox — not
in the code, in the *form*, which is where a lazy `strings` pass won't look.

<Shot src="/shots/phoenix-02-oleid.png" caption="oleid — VBA macros present and flagged HIGH risk, on an MS Word 97-2003 container." wide />

The flow is almost cartoonishly deliberate:

1. Read a hex string out of `UserForm1.TextBox1`.
2. Decode it to bytes.
3. Write it to `C:\Users\Public\hostmanager.txt`.
4. Rename `.txt` → `.png` → `.log` — cycling extensions so nothing watching for a dropped `.exe`
   gets excited.
5. Run it: `cmd.exe /C C:\Users\Public\hostmanager.log`.

So the real payload never touches disk as an executable and never shows up in the macro body. It
lives as hex inside the form. I pulled that stream and carved the PE straight out of it — the form
stored the hex as UTF-16, which is why the stream was 2.5 MB for a 634 KB executable.

<Shot src="/shots/phoenix-03-oledump-streams.png" caption="oledump — the document's streams. Macros/UserForm1/o is 1,268,792 bytes: the hex-encoded payload, sitting in the form rather than the macro body." wide />

That gave me the **stage-2 loader**:

```
Loader   e9a59bbb4e2c28130d4daae12219f7ec2876c66ca9ab7e051cbf587770afab94
Type     64-bit PE, Visual Studio 2022, compiled April 2025
```

<Shot src="/shots/phoenix-04-carve-loader.png" caption="Carving the PE out of the UTF-16 hex stream — 634,368 bytes, MZ confirmed, PE header at offset 256, and the resulting SHA-256." wide />

## Static: a loader that's obviously hiding something

First look at the loader lined up with a packer-style stub. The `.text` section is small (63 KB),
but `.rdata` is huge — 545 KB — and its entropy is **7.36**. High-entropy `.rdata` that big isn't
string tables; it's an encrypted blob. Imports were basically KERNEL32 only, with
`LoadLibrary`/`GetProcAddress` present, which means the real APIs get resolved at runtime.

<Shot src="/shots/phoenix-05-die-entropy.png" caption="DIE reports the loader as packed(92%), and the section view shows .rdata at 7.30 entropy — the embedded encrypted payload." wide />

So: a small loader carrying a big encrypted payload it decrypts and runs. Fine. Now — what's the
cipher? Every report said AES. So I went looking for AES.

## The part where I go looking for AES and don't find it

This is the bit I want to be careful about, because it would be easy to just repeat "AES" and move
on. I didn't want to assume; I wanted to *see* it. AES leaves fingerprints, so I checked for every
one of them:

- **AES-NI** (the CPU doing AES in hardware) — scan for the `AESENC`/`AESDEC`/`AESKEYGENASSIST`
  opcodes. Nothing.
- **Software AES tables** — the forward S-box, the inverse S-box, the T-tables. Nothing.
- **Windows crypto** — `BCrypt*` (CNG) or `CryptDecrypt` (legacy), and the `CALG_AES_256` constant.
  Nothing.
- **ChaCha**, in case it was mislabeled — its `"expand 32-byte k"` constant. Nothing.

<Shot caption="Crypto-triage output — no AES-NI opcodes, no S-box, no CALG constants, no BCrypt imports." />

High entropy fooled the assumption. A 7.36-entropy blob *looks* AES-encrypted, but RC4, a custom
stream cipher, or plain XOR against a long key all look identical on an entropy meter. So instead of
guessing, I opened the loader in Ghidra and read the thing that actually touches the blob.

## Reading the loader instead of guessing it

The loader's `main` was small enough to read top to bottom. Cleaned up, it does this:

```c
buf = VirtualAlloc(0, size, MEM_COMMIT, PAGE_EXECUTE_READWRITE);
memcpy(buf, &encrypted_blob, size);          // copy .rdata blob
decrypt(buf, size, key);                     // <-- the cipher
destuff(buf, size, &out, &outlen);           // <-- a second layer
entry = load_pe(out);                        // map it, get entrypoint
CreateThread(0, 0, entry, 0, 0, 0);          // run it, in THIS process
WaitForSingleObject(thread, INFINITE);
```

<Shot caption="Ghidra — the decompiled loader main: VirtualAlloc, memcpy, decrypt, destuff, CreateThread." wide />

First thing worth saying: it runs the payload with `CreateThread` **in its own process**. No
injection into a browser, no `WriteProcessMemory` into a signed binary — the exact opposite of
BugSleep, which crawled inside Edge. Same crew, completely different tradecraft. Worth remembering
when you write detections: a rule tuned to Phoenix's delivery would never have caught BugSleep, and
vice-versa.

Then I opened the `decrypt` routine. Here it is, in full:

```c
for (i = 0; i < size; i++)
    data[i] ^= key[i % 0x20];
```

<Shot caption="Ghidra — the XOR decrypt loop, key indexed modulo 0x20." />

<Callout type="finding">
That's it. **Repeating XOR with a 32-byte key.** Not AES. Not RC4. XOR — the first cipher anyone
learns and the one everyone swears real malware doesn't use. The key isn't derived or hidden
either; it's sitting in plaintext at the top of `.data`, a keyboard-mash string a developer clearly
typed once and never thought about again: `edwfwergrtgtrgt5hyhy6hghtbgbt5by`
</Callout>

<Shot caption="The 32-byte XOR key sitting in plaintext at the start of .data." />

There was a second, smaller layer too: after the XOR, a routine walks the buffer keeping 10 bytes
and dropping every 11th. It's a byte-stuffing trick — insert a junk byte every ten and the payload
looks even more random on disk, then strip them back out at runtime. Cute, and it raised the entropy
just enough to sell the "AES" look.

## Turning it into a tool that never runs the malware

One decrypted sample is fine. A tool that decrypts *any* Phoenix loader statically is better. Since
I now knew the whole scheme — XOR-32, then de-stuff — I wrote an extractor that peels the core out
of the loader without executing it. It parses the PE, grabs the key from `.data`, XORs the blob,
de-stuffs it, and drops a clean payload.

<Shot caption="The extractor — recovered a 468 KB x64 PE, with its SHA-256." />

Out came a valid 468 KB x64 PE. A wrong key or wrong algorithm gives you garbage, not a valid PE
header with the correct machine field at the right offset — so the scheme was confirmed by the
output itself.

Then I made it better. The key is per-sample, and so are the offsets, so I wrote a *generic* version
that recovers the key automatically: every Phoenix core is a PE, so its decrypted output has to
start with the standard MSVC DOS header. I use those ~60 known header bytes as a crib to solve the
32-byte key and locate the blob — no reversing needed per sample. It reproduced the hand-derived
result byte-for-byte, and later cracked a second loader I'd never seen.

<Shot caption="The generic extractor auto-recovering the XOR key from an unseen loader." />

## Where the config actually lives

Here's the twist that finally explained the "AES" reports. I scanned the decrypted core for its C2
and found… nothing. No `netivtech`, no URL, no user-agent in plaintext. The config isn't stored in
the file at all — the core **builds it at runtime**, assembling the strings in memory as it runs.

So I detonated the core with FakeNet watching, let it register, then took a full memory dump and
read the config out of the live process:

<Shot caption="FakeNet-NG — the DNS query for netivtech.org coming from the detonated payload." />

<Shot caption="The memory dump — netivtech.org, /register, the WinHTTP user-agent, and the base64 beacon body." wide />

The full config, recovered from memory:

```
C2            netivtech.org           (VT-confirmed malicious)
Register      https://netivtech.org/register
Poll          https://netivtech.org/
User-Agent    Mozilla/5.0 (Windows NT 10.0; Win64; x64) WinHttpClient/1.0
Beacon body   base64( HOST\user:OSver, build:priv )
```

That last one decodes to something like `DESKTOP-HBPB2TF\anas-lab:10.0, 19045:5` — hostname,
username, OS build. Its first check-in literally hands the operator the box's identity. (The
user-agent, by the way, is lifted straight from Microsoft's own WinHTTP sample code — the string
`WinHTTP Example/1.0` is still in the binary. Low effort, high signal for us.)

## So, about that AES

Here's the honest reconciliation, scoped to exactly what I can prove:

- The **loader's payload** is XOR + byte-stuffing. Demonstrated in code and by a working extractor.
  Not AES.
- The **core's config** isn't encrypted with anything — it's assembled at runtime. I checked the
  core the same five ways and found no AES there either.
- The only place AES *could* still be hiding is the **live C2 tasking channel** — the data Phoenix
  encrypts to talk to its operator. I never saw that, because I sinkholed the C2 and it never got
  real tasking. And even that's unlikely: a real AES path would have left `CALG` constants or
  resolvable API names, and there are none.

<Callout type="caveat" title="What I am and am not claiming">
I'm not going to tell you Group-IB was wrong — they may well have analyzed a different build (the
sample's own PDB path shows `phoenixV2`/`phoenixV3` nesting, so this thing gets recompiled a lot).
What I *can* say, with evidence, is that **in this build, there is no AES where the reports put
it.** Forcing my findings to match the published label would be exactly the kind of thing I'm trying
not to do.
</Callout>

## Proving it against samples I didn't cook myself

A finding from one sample is a hypothesis. So I went to MalwareBazaar and pivoted on the developers'
own typo — the `Phoniex` tag (yes, misspelled the same way as their build folder). Two samples: an
EXE and a document, both from a *Hungarian*-lure campaign — different from my Israeli/`netivtech`
one.

<Shot caption="MalwareBazaar — the two samples carrying the actor's misspelled Phoniex tag." />

The EXE (`b99edfb9…`) turned out to be a Phoenix **core** — near-identical section layout to the one
I'd decrypted, same runtime-built config, compiled a couple months apart. An independently-sourced
sibling of my core. That alone cross-validated my whole extraction: two roads, same backdoor.

Then the document. I carved its embedded payload the same way — and it dropped straight out as a
**467,968-byte PE with no XOR packing at all**, whose hash matched `b99edfb9` exactly. So the
Hungarian maldoc doesn't use the packed loader; it **embeds the core directly**, drops it as
`Updater_VB.exe`, and runs it via `ShellExecuteA`.

<Shot caption="The hash match — the payload carved from the Hungarian doc equals b99edfb9 exactly." />

That's a real finding, not trivia: **MuddyWater ships Phoenix two different ways.**

```
Israel / netivtech (mine)   maldoc -> XOR-packed stage-2 loader -> decrypt -> core
Hungary (Phoniex)           maldoc -> core embedded directly, dropped as Updater_VB.exe
```

Same backdoor, two delivery styles — which changes how you have to detect it.

## The detections

Because of those two delivery styles, one rule was never going to be enough. I shipped a set:

- **YARA (loader)** — anchored on the XOR key string *and* the encrypted-blob structure together,
  so neither fires alone. Catches the packed variant.
- **YARA (core)** — the durable one. I diffed two independently-sourced cores to find code they
  share, then *subtracted* everything the loader and the C runtime also contain, so no compiler
  boilerplate leaks in. What's left is Phoenix's own code. It fires on every core I have and stays
  quiet on the loader and on `notepad.exe`. This is the rule that catches the backdoor no matter how
  it's delivered.
- **YARA (memory)** — the decrypted config, for process dumps.
- **Suricata** — DNS and TLS SNI for the C2 (it beacons over HTTPS, so the URI and user-agent live
  at your proxy/TLS-inspection layer, not on the raw wire — I split those correctly instead of
  writing a rule that can't fire).
- **Sigma** — the delivery chain (Office spawning a shell that touches `Users\Public\hostmanager.*`
  or drops `Updater_VB.exe`) and the proxy beacon.

Everything's mapped to ATT&CK and every rule compiles and was validated against real samples, not
just eyeballed. How I built the core rule without accidentally matching every Visual Studio binary
on earth is the subject of [part two](/research/hunting-phoenix).
